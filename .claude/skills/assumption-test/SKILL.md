# Assumption test

Building is the most expensive way to learn whether an idea is right. The aim here is to learn the same thing in days, with something that takes hours to set up.

## Start from one assumption

Write the assumption as a statement that could turn out false, about real behaviour: "Families planning a Goa trip will use a page of organised clips instead of searching YouTube themselves." If the founder gives an idea rather than an assumption, pull out the belief that matters most (the `idea-review` skill does this) and confirm it with them.

One test, one assumption. A test that tries to answer three questions usually answers none.

## Pick the lightest method that fits

Listed from cheapest to most costly. Go down the list only as far as needed.

| Method | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Past-behaviour interviews** (5–10 people) | Whether the problem is real and how people cope today | Asking what they would do; pitching the idea |
| **Short survey** | Sizing how common something is, after interviews | Reading opinions as commitments |
| **Fake door or waitlist** | Whether a promise attracts people | Sign-ups are cheap; count a stronger action if possible |
| **Concierge trial** (do it by hand for a few people) | Whether the outcome is valued, before automating | It does not prove it can be automated or afforded |
| **Clickable mock-up test** | Whether people understand and can use a design | It does not show they want it |
| **Small live feature** | Real usage and return visits | Only after the cheaper options have been used |

Prefer evidence of what people do over what they say. A person giving their school calendar, a phone number, or twenty minutes is stronger evidence than a five-star rating.

## Set the thresholds first

Before anything is sent, write down what result means yes, what means no, and what happens in each case. Deciding afterwards makes every result look like support. With the small numbers available here (tens of people, not thousands), state results as counts ("6 of 9"), not percentages, and treat them as direction, not proof.

## What to produce

A one-page plan the founder can run without further help:

1. **Assumption** — the one sentence.
2. **Method and why** — including why a cheaper one would not do.
3. **Who and how many** — and how to reach them. For Ghoomlo this is usually WhatsApp groups of parents, colleagues and neighbours; aim for more than one city.
4. **Exactly what to do** — the questions, page copy, or steps, ready to paste. Interview questions ask about a specific past trip; see the rules in `idea-review/references/lenses.md`, lens 7.
5. **Yes / no thresholds and the decision each leads to.**
6. **Cost and time** — and anything that needs the founder's account, money, or a legal check.
7. **What this will not tell us.**

Save it under `research/` and update the idea's status in `research/idea-log.md` to `testing`.

## Guardrails

- Do not fake a product claim to real people in a way that harms them: a waitlist page must not take money or promise prices that do not exist.
- Collect the least personal data that answers the question, say how it will be used, and delete contact details when the test ends.
- Tools the founder already uses: Maze (free plan: ten question blocks, no website-test block) and Microsoft Clarity on the live site. `research/maze-study-01.md` shows the format that worked.
