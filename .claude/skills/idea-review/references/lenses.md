# The lenses, with questions and sources

These are aids to judgment. None of them produces an answer on its own, and a consumer travel product in India will not match every assumption their authors made.

## 1. Problem quality (Kevin Hale, Y Combinator)

Start from the problem, not the solution. Ask whether the problem is:

- **Popular** — do many people have it?
- **Growing** — is the number of people with it rising?
- **Urgent** — do they need it solved now?
- **Expensive** — does it cost them real money or time?
- **Mandatory** — do they have to solve it?
- **Frequent** — does it come up often?

A good problem scores well on several, rarely all. For Ghoomlo, "frequent" is the usual weak spot.

Source: Kevin Hale, "How to Evaluate Startup Ideas", YC Startup School — [YC recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky).

## 2. Job to be done (Clayton Christensen, Bob Moesta)

People "hire" a product to make progress in a specific situation. Ask:

- What was happening in their life when they went looking?
- What do they use today, and what is wrong with it?
- **Push:** what frustrates them about the current way?
- **Pull:** what attracts them to the new way?
- **Anxiety:** what worries them about switching?
- **Habit:** what keeps them doing what they do now?

A switch happens only when push and pull together beat anxiety and habit.

Source: [Christensen Institute, Jobs to Be Done](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/jobs-to-be-done/).

## 3. Four risks (Marty Cagan, SVPG)

- **Value** — will people choose to use it, or pay for it?
- **Usability** — can they work out how to use it?
- **Feasibility** — can we build it with the time, skills, data and rights we have?
- **Viability** — does it work for the business: cost, revenue, legal, partners?

Teresa Torres adds a fifth worth asking every time: **ethical** — could it cause harm?

For Ghoomlo, feasibility and viability often hinge on things outside the code: price-data partners, content rights for creator videos, and the cost of AI calls.

Sources: [SVPG, product risk taxonomies](https://svpg.com/product-risk-taxonomies); [Teresa Torres, five types of assumptions](https://www.producttalk.org/five-types-of-assumptions/).

## 4. Tarpit check (Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel, Y Combinator)

A tarpit idea looks easy and obviously useful, many founders have tried it, and few got anywhere. Most are consumer ideas. Trip planning is often put in this group: trips are rare, group taste is hard to satisfy, and the inspiration-and-itinerary stage is the most crowded part of travel.

It is not a ban. Ask:

- Who has tried this, and why did they stall?
- What is different now — new technology, new behaviour, a new channel — that makes it possible when it was not before?
- If nothing is different, why would this attempt end differently?

Sources: [Lenny's Newsletter interview with Dalton Caldwell](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups); [Phocuswright, "Why Trip Planning Startups Stumble"](https://www.phocuswright.com/Events/Webinars/Tripped-Up-Why-Trip-Planning-Startups-Stumble); [PhocusWire, "Can trip planning startups ever succeed"](https://www.phocuswire.com/opinion-can-trip-planning-startups-ever-succeed).

## 5. Frequency and retention

- How many times a year does one household have this need?
- What would bring them back between trips?
- If nothing does, each trip means winning the customer again, which makes acquisition expensive.

A simple check once there are users is Sean Ellis's question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this?" If about 40% or more answer "very disappointed", the product is probably something people need. It is a rule of thumb, not a law.

Source: [Rahul Vohra on the product-market fit engine](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/product-market-fit-engine/).

## 6. Defensibility (Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers; Gibson Biddle's DHM)

Ask whether the idea, once working, would **delight** customers in a way that is **hard to copy** and **improves margins**. The durable sources of advantage are: scale economies, network effects, counter-positioning (doing what an incumbent cannot copy without hurting itself), switching costs, brand, a cornered resource, and process.

An early product rarely has any of these. The useful question is which one this idea could grow into, for example household context that builds up over years (switching costs) or observed price history (a resource others lack).

Sources: Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers* (2016) — [summary](https://evansamek.substack.com/p/summary-7-powers); [Gibson Biddle's DHM model](https://www.airfocus.com/glossary/dhm-product-strategy/).

## 7. Evidence (Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test)

Sort what we know by strength:

1. What people did (booked, paid, came back).
2. What people say they did on a specific past occasion.
3. What people say they would do.
4. What we believe.

Opinions about the future and compliments count for little. When talking to people: talk about their life, not our idea; ask about specific past events; listen more than talk.

Source: [notes on The Mom Test](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/).
