# Idea review — tool for travel agents, and school-holiday fare watch

**Date:** 16 November 2026. **Evidence available:** one founder interview; Maze study 01 has drawn few responses. No conversations with travel agents yet. Reasoning, not proof.

## A — tool for small travel agents

*For small Indian travel agents, when a customer enquiry arrives, Ghoomlo turns it into a branded day-by-day proposal page with maps in minutes, so they reply faster and win more bookings.*

**Verdict: mixed.** Agents are a real, paying market and they plan trips every day. It is also a crowded space, and it turns the work into selling to businesses.

Variants:
1. **Proposal builder** — the standard product; several exist.
2. **Enquiry-to-proposal assistant** — paste a WhatsApp enquiry, get a draft proposal. The wedge is speed.
3. **Live trip page for the agent's customer** — a shareable page with days and maps in place of a PDF, under the agent's name. Closest to what is built.
4. **Specialist plans** — Jain or vegetarian, seniors, pilgrimage groups, for agents who serve those communities.

Strong: frequency (many trips a week per agent); offline channels still carry a large share of Indian travel bookings (about $17.8bn of $41.7bn in 2025 per Phocuswright); agents already pay for software (one listing shows TravClan from about ₹20,000 a year); the plan, map and print output exist.

Weak or unknown: crowded (Sembark, TravClan, Adivaha, Toursoft, TripCreator all offer itinerary and quotation builders); an agent's proposal is mostly prices and supplier rates, which Ghoomlo does not have; the founder is a traveller, not an agent, so access and credibility have to be built; selling to small businesses is slow, hands-on work.

| Lens | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Problem quality | Unknown | Frequent for agents; whether proposals are a painful step is unverified |
| Job to be done | Mixed | "Reply to this enquiry fast with something that wins the booking" — plausible, unconfirmed |
| Four risks | Mixed | Value unknown; viability better than consumer; feasibility gap is pricing data |
| Tarpit check | Mixed | Not a consumer tarpit, but many incumbents |
| Frequency | Strong | Daily use |
| Defensibility | Weak | Features are easy to match; an edge would have to come from a niche |
| Evidence | Weak | Market figures only; no agent has been asked |

**Riskiest assumption:** small agents have a proposal pain that their current tools and WhatsApp habits do not solve, and would switch for it.

**Cheapest test:** talk to five to eight small agents and watch each build one real quote. Then, for two of them, hand-make three proposal pages from real enquiries using the current site. Yes: at least one sends a page to a customer unprompted and asks for more. No: they say their current tool or Word template is fine, or that price is the only thing customers read.

## B — school-holiday fare watch

*For families in one city, Ghoomlo sends a short weekly digest of the best fares for upcoming school breaks and long weekends, so they book before prices climb.*

**Verdict: promising, and the cheapest thing on the list to test.** The worry is revenue, not demand.

Variants:
1. **City digest** — one WhatsApp channel per city: "Christmas break, Bengaluru to Goa, seen at ₹X on [date], [site]".
2. **Personal watch** — a family names dates and places; they get alerts for those.
3. **Holiday calendar** — the year's school breaks and long weekends from one city, with typical fare levels; a content and search play.
4. **Paid membership** — the model Going uses in the US, where members pay a yearly fee.

Strong: no code and no price-data partner needed to start, since a person can check public fare sites and write the digest; it fits the pain in the evidence (booking late, paying more); weekly contact year-round; parent WhatsApp groups are a natural way to spread; it is the first slice of the year-ahead planner.

Weak or unknown: generic fare alerts are free from airlines and booking sites, so the value has to come from the school-calendar fit and the curation; flight referrals pay little and Indian families' willingness to pay a membership is unknown; hand curation does not scale and automating it brings back the price-data dependency; a stale or wrong fare damages trust quickly.

| Lens | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Problem quality | Mixed to strong | Recurring, costs real money, tied to fixed dates |
| Job to be done | Strong | "Tell me when to book for the break we already have" |
| Four risks | Mixed | Value plausible; feasible by hand; viability is the open question |
| Tarpit check | Mixed | Alerts exist; the calendar-and-family angle is the difference |
| Frequency | Strong | Weekly |
| Defensibility | Weak at first | An audience and observed price history could build over time |
| Evidence | Weak | Fits one interview; untested |

**Riskiest assumption:** families will act on a curated alert, not just read it.

**Cheapest test:** run a free WhatsApp channel for one city for four weeks, one hand-written digest a week, each fare shown with where and when it was seen. Yes: 50 or more people join from a few parent groups, and at least five reply or say they booked, shortlisted or forwarded it. No: few join, or they join and nobody responds.

## Comparison

| | Agents tool | Fare watch |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first signal | 2–3 weeks of conversations | 1 week to start, 4 to read |
| Needs code | No (uses the current site) | No |
| Who pays | Agents, plausibly | Unclear |
| Main risk | Crowded; selling to agents | Thin revenue |
| Fit with what is known | New territory | Matches the evidence |

**Suggested order:** start the fare watch now, because it costs almost nothing and feeds the year-ahead planner. Hold five agent conversations alongside it, as listening only. Decide on the agents direction after hearing them.
