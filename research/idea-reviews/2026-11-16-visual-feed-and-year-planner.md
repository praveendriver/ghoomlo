# Idea review — visual feed, and year-ahead planner

**Date:** 16 November 2026. **Evidence available:** one founder interview (N=1) in `DISCOVERY_NOTES.md`; Maze study 01 is out and no results are back. Everything below is reasoning, not proof.

## Idea 1 — the visual feed

*For people choosing what to do on a trip, Ghoomlo gathers the best vlog, blog and influencer content into categories and shows it as pictures and video with very little text, so they can decide quickly.*

**Verdict: mixed.** It is the more exciting product to look at and the weaker business. It also pulls slightly against the pain it is meant to solve.

**Strong**
- It aims at a pain the interview named: hours spent watching vlogs.
- A feed gives people a reason to open the product between trips, which a planner does not.
- It would look and feel unlike a chatbot, which matters for standing out.

**Weak or unknown**
- **Rights.** The content belongs to creators and lives on YouTube, Instagram and TikTok. Showing it means using each platform's official embeds under their terms, which limit how a player can be presented; copying clips onto our own servers is not an option. A full-screen, swipeable feed of other people's videos may not be allowed in the form imagined. This needs checking against each platform's current terms before any design work.
- **It may be more video, not less.** The interview said the hard part is *synthesis*: finding what many vloggers agree on and where they differ. A feed with minimal text hands the watching back to the user. The value is in the organising, and organising needs some words.
- **Easy to copy, hard to defend.** Instagram and YouTube own both the content and the habit, and are free to add trip-planning features themselves.
- **"Minimal text" strains the trust rules.** Labels such as "sponsored", "filmed in 2023" and "not verified" are text.

| Lens | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Problem quality | Mixed | Common and time-consuming, but not urgent or mandatory |
| Job to be done | Mixed | The job is "tell me what is worth doing"; a feed shows rather than tells |
| Four risks | Weak on feasibility | Content rights and platform terms are the main risk; value unknown |
| Tarpit check | Weak | Inspiration is the most crowded part of travel |
| Frequency | Strong | Browsable between trips |
| Defensibility | Weak | Others own the content and the audience |
| Evidence | Unknown | One interview; nothing on whether people prefer clips to a digest |

**Riskiest assumption:** people want an organised set of clips more than a written synthesis, and we can show those clips within platform rules.

**Cheapest test:** build one destination page by hand (Goa): 25–30 clips using official embeds that start at the relevant moment, sorted into food, beaches, sights and "things people disagree on", with one line of text each. Give it to ten people who are planning Goa. Yes: at least six say it replaced their own YouTube searching and can name a decision it changed. No: most still go and search themselves, or ask for a summary instead.

## Idea 2 — the year-ahead planner

*For families, Ghoomlo takes the year's school holidays, leave, budget and wishes, lays out which trips to take and when, watches prices, and adjusts the plan as things change.*

**Verdict: promising, with one hard dependency.** It fixes the biggest structural weakness of a trip planner and matches the strongest thing in the evidence. It cannot fully work without price data we do not yet have.

**Strong**
- **It solves the frequency problem.** A single trip is planned a few times a year; a year plan is a standing relationship with reasons to return (alerts, changes, the next window).
- **It matches what happened.** The interview's clearest story was booking late because dates were unsettled and work got busy, then paying more. A plan made ahead, with nudges, addresses exactly that.
- **The value is money.** Booking school-holiday travel early can save a meaningful amount, which is easier to charge for than convenience.
- **It builds something hard to copy.** A household's calendar, constraints and history grow more useful each year, and observed prices over time are a resource a newcomer lacks.
- **It is what the founder already built for themselves.** The earlier personal system centres on holiday dates, fare tracking and budgets.

**Weak or unknown**
- **Price data.** Watching prices needs a provider who permits it. Without one, the product can lay out the year and remind people to check, but cannot watch for them.
- **Do families plan this far ahead?** Some plan around school holidays; many decide a few weeks out. Unknown, and decisive.
- **It asks for a lot up front.** Calendar, leave and budget are more than the light start just built. It would need to earn each piece gradually.
- **"Reconfigure automatically" is hard to do well** and risky to get wrong; early versions should suggest, not change.

| Lens | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Problem quality | Mixed to strong | Expensive when missed and recurring every year; not urgent on any given day |
| Job to be done | Strong | "Make sure we take the good trips this year without overpaying or missing the window" |
| Four risks | Mixed | Value plausible; feasibility and viability depend on a price partner |
| Tarpit check | Mixed | Not an itinerary generator; closer to fare-alert services, which have worked as subscriptions elsewhere |
| Frequency | Strong | Year-round by design |
| Defensibility | Mixed to strong | Household context and price history accumulate |
| Evidence | Weak | Fits one interview well; nothing from other families yet |

**Riskiest assumption:** families will plan months ahead and share their calendar, when most plan trip by trip.

**Cheapest test:** do it by hand for five to eight families. Collect school holidays, leave and a rough budget in a short form; send each a one-page "your 2027 travel calendar" with the best windows; then send manual price check-ins by WhatsApp for four to six weeks. Yes: most complete the form, at least half act on a window or an alert (shortlist, book, or ask for more), and some say what they would pay. No: people do not fill in the calendar, or read the plan and do nothing.

## Together

They fit as one product with a spine and a surface: the year plan decides *when* and keeps people returning; the visual content helps choose *where* and *what* inside each window. The year plan is the part that could be a business; the visual layer is what makes it pleasant.

**Suggested order:** test the year-ahead planner first, by hand. Test the visual idea as a single destination page, after checking platform terms. Build neither until one test comes back yes.

**On Maze study 01:** its ranking question covers "best-price dates", "vlogger summary" and "family plan". That speaks to parts of both ideas but not to planning a whole year or to a video-first format, so read its results as a first signal only.
